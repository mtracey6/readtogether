Plan one rigorous lesson for the language levels actually in the room.

ELD AI: Read Together is a working lesson-planning prototype shaped by eight years in K–5 classrooms. It turns a teacher's grade, subject, topic, Key Language Use, and WIDA levels into an editable scaffold preview while keeping student information out of the model payload. This is a product and instructional-design demonstration—not a research study or a claim of classroom impact.

01  The gap

The classroom problem: differentiation is necessary, repetitive, and time-constrained.

A mixed-proficiency classroom may share one content objective while requiring different amounts of language support. ReadTogether began as a teacher-designed exploration of how to make that planning work more structured without replacing the educator's knowledge of students, curriculum, IEPs, language-support plans, or local requirements.

RigorKeep the grade-level content goal stable while changing the amount of language support.
ClarityMake objectives, vocabulary, sentence frames, and formative checks easy to inspect and edit.
JudgmentTreat AI output as a draft for teacher review, never as a curriculum or assessment authority.

Design reference. WIDA ELD Standards Framework informs the prototype's Key Language Uses and proficiency-level scaffolds. The project is independent and has not been reviewed or endorsed by WIDA.

02  The product

A working teacher workflow, with future concepts kept clearly separate.

The implemented portfolio surface focuses on teacher planning. A bilingual family communication concept is included to show product direction, but there is no learner profile, assessment engine, classroom integration, or participant evidence behind it.

Surface A · Teacher

Differentiated planning in about a minute

Grade, subject, topic, the WIDA levels actually sitting in the room, and one of the four Key Language Uses. Out comes a plan with paired content and language objectives, tiered vocabulary, sentence frames written separately for each level present, and a formative check.

  • Scaffolds by level — never a simplified text for everyone
  • Tier 1 / 2 / 3 vocabulary split, not one undifferentiated word list
  • Teacher review required before classroom use; the prototype places the disclaimer with the plan
  • Designed to support saving, editing, and school-level sharing
Future concept · Family communication

Explain the learning focus in plain language

The family-view mockup explores how a teacher might share a learning focus, a plain-language explanation, and practical at-home suggestions in English and Spanish. It deliberately avoids simulated scores or student records.

  • Authored bilingual interface copy, not runtime machine translation

  • No assumption that a caregiver reads English or owns special materials
  • Clearly labeled as a future concept, not a working school communication system

04  What lands in the teacher's hands

A plan, not a wall of text.

Generated output is structured before it is written, so it can be saved, edited, filtered by level, and reused. Below is a designed output shape for a mixed-level third grade science block.

Generated plan

The Water Cycle — Grade 3 Science

50 minutes · Key Language Use: Explain · WIDA levels present: 1, 2, 3, 4

Draft
Review before classroom use. This plan is AI-generated. Check it against your district's curriculum, your students' IEP and language support plans, and your own read of the room. The teacher is the professional in the loop; the model is not.

Objectives

Content: Students explain how water moves through evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection.

Language: Students explain cause-and-effect relationships in the water cycle using level-appropriate frames and Tier 3 vocabulary, with visual and peer support.

Lesson arc

  1. 0–8 minAnchor. Show the labeled cycle diagram. Everyone gets the same diagram. Levels 1–2 receive it with picture-word cards; levels 3–4 receive it unlabeled and label it themselves.
  2. 8–20 minVocabulary. Introduce four Tier 3 terms with gesture, image, and cognate bridge (evaporación / evaporation). Students build a personal glossary entry.
  3. 20–38 minExplain in pairs. Mixed-level partners. Each student uses their own frame from the ladder above. The level 1 partner speaks first with the word bank — going first protects them from being talked over.
  4. 38–48 minWrite. Same task, differentiated output: label a diagram, write a sentence, write a causal chain, or write a paragraph with transitions.
  5. 48–50 minExit check. One sentence: why do clouds form? Collected, sorted by level, used to regroup tomorrow.

Formative assessment

Score language production against the frame the student was given, not against a single grade-level target. A level 2 student producing a correct simple sentence with the right Tier 3 term has met the objective. Record the level, not just right or wrong — that is what drives the next plan.

Materials

Water cycle diagram (labeled + unlabeled), Tier 3 picture-word cards, sentence frame strips by level, personal glossary page, exit slip.

Family update · interface concept

Designed interface copy · no learner data

Our learning focus: explaining cause and effect

Focusexplain why something happens
Wordsbecause · so · as a result
Trytell the idea in any language first

What this means: The class is practicing how to connect a cause with its effect. The goal is to explain the relationship clearly; using the home language to rehearse the idea is welcome.

How to help at home

  1. Read together in any language. Reading in Spanish builds the same skills English reading needs.
  2. Ask: “Why did that happen?” and “What happened next?” during a story or daily routine.
  3. Use words such as because, so, and as a result when you explain an idea together.

Spanish copy is authored, not machine-translated at runtime. Tips avoid assuming a home library, a quiet room, or a caregiver who reads English — the three assumptions most family engagement content makes by default.

06  How it's built

The privacy boundary is an architectural object, not a policy paragraph.

In the proposed architecture, student records and the model sit on opposite sides of an explicit boundary. A model payload is constructed field by field—an allow-list, not a redaction pass. The API prototype implements this boundary; object-level authorization and vendor behavior remain pre-pilot verification gates.

Runtime

Bun end to end. TypeScript strict. Single API surface, so the allow-list filter has exactly one place to live and one place to be tested.

Data

The prototype uses SQLite. A real pilot would require verified encrypted storage, retention and deletion controls, backups, and a documented migration path before multi-school use.

Model boundary

The lesson-generation request accepts only instructional parameters. Learner names, identifiers, rosters, audio, service status, IEP information, and open-ended notes are outside the proposed payload.

07  Compliance posture

Designed around the questions a reviewer should ask.

A school technology review should establish what data leaves the school, who can access records, how consent works, and what gets deleted. The statements below are proposed requirements for a pilot architecture—not certifications or claims about an operating service.

FERPA

Proposed requirement: education records remain in a school-controlled environment; teacher and parent access is role- and relationship-scoped; object-level authorization is tested for every record endpoint; disclosure is limited by written agreement and service purpose.

COPPA

Proposed requirement: any future child-facing access would be provisioned through an approved school or parent consent flow, not independent child sign-up. The current public demonstration is a teacher-planning surface and does not collect child information.

AI use disclosure

The prototype displays a review-before-use disclaimer beside designed lesson output. Generated material remains an editable suggestion; educators must check it against curriculum, standards, accommodations, language-support plans, and the learners in front of them.

What's deliberately absent

No learner accounts, roster import, assessment score, progress dashboard, advertising, data sale, or third-party analytics are presented as working features in this public portfolio build.

Design references. WIDA ELD Standards Framework · NIST AI Risk Management Framework · OWASP API object-level authorization guidance. These references inform the design; they do not certify the prototype.

08  Where this actually is

This is a designed prototype, not a validated product.

As of today: zero research participants, zero classrooms, zero students, and zero outcome data. No child, educator, family, school, or district has evaluated this design. What follows is a proposed path to evaluation—not a completed case study.

Why say that out loud. A portfolio should separate evidence from intent. This project demonstrates product framing, instructional design, risk management, and implementation choices; it does not demonstrate usability, adoption, learning impact, or recognition accuracy. Known risks and what happens if they hit

Generated scaffolds are instructionally weak

If: qualified educators find that objectives, vocabulary, or frames do not fit the selected grade, content, and language context.
Then: revise the prompt and output schema—or remove generation—before asking teachers to depend on it.

Instructional input leaks learner information

If: testing finds that a model request can include names, identifiers, rosters, service status, or open-ended learner notes.
Then: block release and repair the allow-list boundary before any school evaluation.

A standards framework is treated as a universal recipe

If: teachers cannot adapt the output to local curriculum, state requirements, or individual supports.
Then: make those constraints explicit inputs and keep every output editable by default.

Teachers over-trust AI plans

If: plans get used unmodified.
Then: that is a design failure, not a user failure. Mitigations: mandatory review disclaimer, editable-by-default output, and interviews that specifically probe what teachers changed and why.
