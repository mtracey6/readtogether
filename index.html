She read it right. The test marked it wrong.

Oral-reading tools can confound pronunciation variation with decoding errors when they are not designed and evaluated with multilingual speakers. For the 5.3 million English learners in U.S. public schools, that is a product risk worth testing—not an outcome I am claiming to have solved. ELD AI: Read Together explores a bilingual, WIDA-aligned approach to lesson planning and reading practice, with explicit safeguards and evaluation criteria designed before any participant research begins.

01  The gap

The product risk: pronunciation variation can be mistaken for decoding error.

Timed oral-reading measures are commonly administered one-on-one and require educators to distinguish decoding errors from pronunciation differences. ReadTogether began as a design hypothesis: could a tool preserve teacher judgment, make transfer patterns visible, and reduce repetitive scoring work without presenting an automated score as ground truth? No participant research has yet tested that hypothesis.

02  The product

One language model of the learner. Two surfaces.

The product concept holds a single representation of each student's language proficiency — WIDA level, key language use, L1 background — and spends it in two directions: planning for the teacher, and practice plus assessment for the child. Neither surface works without the other.

Surface A · Teacher

Differentiated planning in about a minute

Grade, subject, topic, the WIDA levels actually sitting in the room, and one of the four Key Language Uses. Out comes a plan with paired content and language objectives, tiered vocabulary, sentence frames written separately for each level present, and a formative check.

  • Scaffolds by level — never a simplified text for everyone
  • Tier 1 / 2 / 3 vocabulary split, not one undifferentiated word list
  • Teacher review required before classroom use; the prototype places the disclaimer with the plan
  • Designed to support saving, editing, and school-level sharing
Surface B · Student & family

Reading practice that listens fairly

Phonics-aligned decodable passages, audio support, and oral reading capture that scores rate and accuracy while holding L1 phonological transfer separate from decoding error. Progress lands in a parent dashboard that works in Spanish.

  • Decodability self-audit against the taught phonics inventory
  • Comprehension items with explained feedback, not just right/wrong
  • Authored Spanish family-view prototype, not runtime machine translation
  • Text-to-speech, high contrast, and adjustable text specified as accessibility requirements

04  What lands in the teacher's hands

A plan, not a wall of text.

Generated output is structured before it is written, so it can be saved, edited, filtered by level, and reused. Below is a designed output shape for a mixed-level third grade science block.

Generated plan

The Water Cycle — Grade 3 Science

50 minutes · Key Language Use: Explain · WIDA levels present: 1, 2, 3, 4

Draft
Review before classroom use. This plan is AI-generated. Check it against your district's curriculum, your students' IEP and language support plans, and your own read of the room. The teacher is the professional in the loop; the model is not.

Objectives

Content: Students explain how water moves through evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection.

Language: Students explain cause-and-effect relationships in the water cycle using level-appropriate frames and Tier 3 vocabulary, with visual and peer support.

Lesson arc

  1. 0–8 minAnchor. Show the labeled cycle diagram. Everyone gets the same diagram. Levels 1–2 receive it with picture-word cards; levels 3–4 receive it unlabeled and label it themselves.
  2. 8–20 minVocabulary. Introduce four Tier 3 terms with gesture, image, and cognate bridge (evaporación / evaporation). Students build a personal glossary entry.
  3. 20–38 minExplain in pairs. Mixed-level partners. Each student uses their own frame from the ladder above. The level 1 partner speaks first with the word bank — going first protects them from being talked over.
  4. 38–48 minWrite. Same task, differentiated output: label a diagram, write a sentence, write a causal chain, or write a paragraph with transitions.
  5. 48–50 minExit check. One sentence: why do clouds form? Collected, sorted by level, used to regroup tomorrow.

Formative assessment

Score language production against the frame the student was given, not against a single grade-level target. A level 2 student producing a correct simple sentence with the right Tier 3 term has met the objective. Record the level, not just right or wrong — that is what drives the next plan.

Materials

Water cycle diagram (labeled + unlabeled), Tier 3 picture-word cards, sentence frame strips by level, personal glossary page, exit slip.

05  The scoring decision

An accent is not a decoding error.

A Spanish-dominant second grader who says estop for stop has not misread the word. She has applied a phonological rule from her first language, on a word she decoded correctly. Legacy scorers count it as an error anyway. That single design choice is the difference between “at benchmark” and “refer for intervention.”

Working scorer Runs in your browser. Deterministic rules, no model call.

Benchmark reference: grade 2 winter 50th-percentile oral reading fluency sits near 72 WCPM (Hasbrouck & Tindal oral reading fluency norms). Accuracy is words correct over words attempted; self-corrections and repetitions are not scored as errors, following running-record convention.

Where this is still fragile. The pattern layer is only as good as the speech recognition underneath it, and general-purpose ASR is trained disproportionately on adult native speakers. A future study would audit recognition accuracy separately for each first-language group; if the gap between groups exceeds 10 percentage points the model gets replaced, not tuned. This is stated as a known risk rather than a solved problem, because it isn't solved.

07  How it's built

The privacy boundary is an architectural object, not a policy paragraph.

In the proposed architecture, student records and the model sit on opposite sides of an explicit boundary. A model payload is constructed field by field—an allow-list, not a redaction pass. The API prototype implements this boundary; object-level authorization and vendor behavior remain pre-pilot verification gates.

Runtime

Bun end to end. TypeScript strict. Single API surface, so the allow-list filter has exactly one place to live and one place to be tested.

Data

The prototype uses SQLite. A real pilot would require verified encrypted storage, retention and deletion controls, backups, and a documented migration path before multi-school use.

Audio

The product requirement is to keep voice out of the LLM path and avoid retention. The current browser speech-recognition concept has not been approved for student use; vendor processing must be evaluated before research.

08  Compliance posture

Designed around the questions a reviewer should ask.

A school technology review should establish what data leaves the school, who can access records, how consent works, and what gets deleted. The statements below are proposed requirements for a pilot architecture—not certifications or claims about an operating service.

FERPA

Proposed requirement: education records remain in a school-controlled environment; teacher and parent access is role- and relationship-scoped; object-level authorization is tested for every record endpoint; disclosure is limited by written agreement and service purpose.

COPPA

Proposed requirement: student access is provisioned through an approved school or parent consent flow, not independent child sign-up. Voice is treated as personal information, making the recognition-vendor decision a legal and privacy review—not only an accuracy choice.

AI use disclosure

The prototype displays a review-before-use disclaimer. Before any student-facing generation, a decodability self-audit against the taught phonics inventory would be a release gate.

What's deliberately absent

No advertising, no data sale, no third-party analytics on student surfaces, no engagement mechanics designed to extend session time. Gamification is capped at streaks and badges tied to real reading milestones.

Design references. WIDA ELD Standards Framework · NIST AI Risk Management Framework · OWASP API object-level authorization guidance. These references inform the design; they do not certify the prototype.

09  Where this actually is

This is a designed prototype, not a validated product.

As of today: zero research participants, zero classrooms, zero students, and zero outcome data. No child, educator, family, school, or district has evaluated this design. What follows is a proposed evaluation plan—not a completed case study—written down before research so the criteria cannot quietly move afterward.

Why say that out loud. A portfolio should separate evidence from intent. This project demonstrates product framing, instructional design, risk management, and implementation choices; it does not demonstrate usability, adoption, learning impact, or recognition accuracy. Known risks and what happens if they hit

Speech recognition bias against multilingual speakers

If: the accuracy gap between L1 groups exceeds 10 points in the ASR audit.
Then: replace the ASR model with a multilingual-trained alternative before any score is shown to a teacher. Scores are withheld, not shipped with a caveat.

Child voice data as a privacy surface

If: the chosen ASR path routes audio to a third party without a signed student-data agreement.
Then: move to on-device or self-hosted recognition, accepting an accuracy cost. Voice is personal information; convenience does not outrank that.

Generated passages break the phonics scope and sequence

If: a passage contains graphemes the class has not been taught.
Then: the decodability audit rejects and regenerates before the passage is ever shown. The audit runs on generation, not on review.

Teachers over-trust AI plans

If: plans get used unmodified.
Then: that is a design failure, not a user failure. Mitigations: mandatory review disclaimer, editable-by-default output, and interviews that specifically probe what teachers changed and why.

10  Who built it

Classroom first, then the code.

I am an instructional and learning experience designer transitioning from eight years in K–5 classrooms, with graduate degrees in Educational Technology & Instructional Design and Mathematics Education. ReadTogether grew from a recurring design problem: educators need practical differentiation tools, while multilingual learners need language variation treated as information—not automatically as error. The useful part of this project is not simply the model call; it is the product boundary around it, the instructional decisions inside it, and the discipline to distinguish a prototype from evidence.
